👟 Sportswear Industry

  • Industry chemical-safety baseline released — Nonprofit ChemForward published an industry-wide safer-chemistry baseline built from more than 15,000 manufacturer safety data sheets covering nearly 44,000 substances, developed with Nike, Lululemon, Patagonia and Target; it found 69% of disclosed chemicals were classified as safer substances and 15.8% were chemicals of concern, while about 12% remained undisclosed — the report's key transparency gap. (WWD/Sourcing Journal)
  • New Balance's third McLaughlin-Levrone line — Olympic champion Sydney McLaughlin-Levrone launched her third and most creatively-driven signature collection with New Balance on Sept. 8, an eight-piece assortment spanning three footwear silhouettes ($145–$170) and five apparel pieces, with the athlete taking an active hand in creative direction. (WWD, New Balance)
  • O'Neill takes over at Lululemon — Heidi O'Neill, a 25-year Nike veteran, started as Lululemon's CEO and joined its board Tuesday, immediately facing a quarter that showed North American sales down 12% and leggings sales down 20%, with the company cutting its full-year outlook for the second time in three months. (CNN, The Globe and Mail)
  • Celine and Reebok's $750 sneaker — Celine and Reebok released the reimagined Freestyle Lo — an '80s Reebok icon rebuilt in lambskin leather by Celine creative director Michael Rider — in eight colorways on Sept. 10, marking the French house's first known sneaker collaboration at a $750 retail price. (WWD)

🤝 Community Impact & CSR

  • Portland Fruit Tree Project's fall harvest — Portland Fruit Tree Project, a 20-year-old nonprofit, harvests edible fruit from thousands of Portland-metro backyards, community gardens and parking-strip trees and redistributes it to people facing food insecurity, as staff and volunteers ramp up for the fall harvest season. (OPB)
  • Anonymous $50M gift to Americares — An unidentified Connecticut family gave global-health nonprofit Americares a record $50 million — its largest gift ever — in response to U.S. foreign-aid cuts that hit programs including a maternal-care project in Tanzania, migrant health clinics in Colombia, and a shelved five-year Gaza medical-supply effort; the family has supported the group for 30 years. (Chronicle of Philanthropy, CT Mirror)
  • Grocery chain's school drive tops $313K — Texas-based Brookshire Grocery Co. wrapped its third annual "Supplies for Schools" checkout-donation campaign, raising more than $313,000 for local schools for the 2026–27 year, more than double the prior year's total, with funds staying in the communities where they were raised. (Mass Market Retailers — company announcement)

🌲 Portland

  • I-5 Rose Quarter closure begins tonight — ODOT's five-week, round-the-clock closure of I-5 southbound between I-405 and I-84 begins at 10 p.m. Friday, sending an estimated 120,000 daily drivers — including nearly 11,700 trucks — onto detours via I-405 and the Broadway/Weidler exit, with officials warning of some of the worst traffic delays in the region's history. (Portland Tribune, KOIN)
  • Council renames Cesar Chavez Boulevard — Portland City Council voted unanimously, 12-0 on second reading, to rename SE César E. Chávez Boulevard to Campesinos Boulevard, following renewed scrutiny this year of sexual-abuse allegations against Chávez; co-sponsor Councilor Loretta Smith said the new name honors farmworkers collectively, and PBOT has up to five years to change signage. (OPB, Portland Tribune)
  • PPS closure plan meets union pushback — As Portland Public Schools weighs closing up to 15–20 elementary and middle schools to close a roughly $50 million budget shortfall amid declining enrollment, the teachers union is mounting fierce resistance, framing the "rightsizing" push as a jobs fight ahead of an expected board vote later this year. (Willamette Week)
  • Longtime mothers' treatment center to close — Central City Concern will permanently close the Letty Owings Center, a 29-bed residential treatment facility for pregnant and parenting women with substance-use disorder operating since 1989, effective Oct. 31, citing financial strain and the added complexity of treating fentanyl use; displaced clients will move to a facility that cannot house children. (KGW)
